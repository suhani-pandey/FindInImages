\section{System Design and Architecture}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Overview}
\proj{} is a \emph{pipeline} that turns a scanned PDF into a searchable copy of
itself and then lets Chrome show that copy. Chrome's native PDF viewer displays
every PDF as usual. When the user invokes the extension on one (keyboard shortcut
or toolbar icon), the service worker hands the job to a hidden offscreen
document. The tab keeps showing the original while the offscreen document
downloads the file, finds the pages that paint images or have no text,
recognizes them with OCR, and writes the recognized words into a copy as
invisible text. The tab is then navigated to the copy, still in Chrome's own
viewer, where Chrome's own $\mathrm{Ctrl}{+}\mathrm{F}$ finds the words.
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the end-to-end flow.

\subsection{Components}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4em, style=nextline]
  \item[Activation and coordination] The service worker responds to the toolbar
        icon or the keyboard shortcut, starts the offscreen document, sends it the
        job, shows progress on the icon's badge, and navigates the tab when the
        copy is ready. Invoked on a copy, it restores the original.
  \item[Offscreen document] A hidden extension page with a DOM. It queues jobs
        and runs the pipeline; nothing in it is ever visible.
  \item[Download and identity] The PDF is fetched again from its URL with the
        user's cookies, checked for the \code{\%PDF-} signature, and identified
        by the SHA-256 of its bytes.
  \item[Text detection] For each page, PDF.js reports existing text and whether
        the page paints raster images.
  \item[OCR] Pages that need it are rendered offscreen at high resolution,
        preprocessed, and recognized by a pool of Tesseract.js workers. Results
        are cached in IndexedDB.
  \item[Mapping and embedding] Recognized word boxes are mapped into PDF user
        space, de-duplicated against existing text, and written into the page as
        invisible text by pdf-lib.
  \item[Delivery] The saved copy goes into Cache Storage; the service worker's
        \code{fetch} handler serves it at an extension URL, and Chrome's viewer
        displays it.
  \item[Search] Chrome's native find, highlighting, selection, and copy---no
        code of ours runs at search time.
\end{description}

\subsection{The Coordinate-Mapping Problem}
\label{sec:coords}
The geometric problem is to place each invisible word so that Chrome's find
highlight lands on the visible word. Chrome draws that highlight from the glyphs'
own geometry---their baseline, font size and advance widths---so the text must be
written at the right position, size, angle, and width in \emph{PDF user space}.
The recognized word, however, starts life in a different space:

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[
    sp/.style={rectangle, rounded corners=2pt, draw=accent!70, fill=accent!6,
               align=center, font=\small, minimum height=10mm,
               inner sep=4pt, text width=2.3cm},
    a/.style={-{Stealth[length=2.2mm]}, draw=accent!80, thick},
    lbl/.style={font=\scriptsize\itshape, align=center}]
  \node[sp] (ocr) {OCR canvas\\\footnotesize pixels, $\downarrow$ origin};
  \node[sp, right=9mm of ocr] (norm) {Normalized\\\footnotesize $[0,1]^2$, cached};
  \node[sp, right=9mm of norm] (vis) {Visual page\\\footnotesize viewport, rotated};
  \node[sp, right=9mm of vis] (pdf) {PDF user space\\\footnotesize points, $\uparrow$ origin};
  \draw[a] (ocr) -- node[lbl,above]{$\div W_{\text{ocr}}$} (norm);
  \draw[a] (norm) -- node[lbl,above]{$\times W,H$} (vis);
  \draw[a] (vis) -- node[lbl,above]{$T_V^{-1}$} (pdf);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

Tesseract reports word boxes $(x_0,y_0,x_1,y_1)$ in pixels of the OCR canvas.
These are first normalized by the canvas size,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{x} = \frac{x}{W_{\text{ocr}}},\qquad
  \tilde{y} = \frac{y}{H_{\text{ocr}}},
  \label{eq:normalize}
\end{equation}
so that cached results do not depend on the resolution at which OCR ran.

The OCR canvas was rendered through a PDF.js viewport, whose affine transform
$T_V$ maps PDF user space to the \emph{visual} page (rotation and the flip from a
bottom-left to a top-left origin included). Let $W \times H$ be that page's size
at scale~1. The word's baseline is taken at $80\%$ of its box height, and four
points are mapped back with the inverse transform $T_V^{-1}$ (PDF.js
\code{convertToPdfPoint}):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  y_b &= \bigl(\tilde{y}_0 + 0.8\,(\tilde{y}_1-\tilde{y}_0)\bigr)\,H, \\
  S &= T_V^{-1}(\tilde{x}_0 W,\; y_b), &
  E &= T_V^{-1}(\tilde{x}_1 W,\; y_b), \\
  P_t &= T_V^{-1}(\tilde{x}_0 W,\; \tilde{y}_0 H), &
  P_b &= T_V^{-1}(\tilde{x}_0 W,\; \tilde{y}_1 H).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:points}
\end{equation}
$S$ and $E$ are the baseline's start and end in PDF space, and $P_t$, $P_b$ the
box's top and bottom. From them follow the word's width, its font size, and its
baseline angle:
\begin{equation}
  w = \lVert E - S \rVert, \qquad
  h = \lVert P_b - P_t \rVert, \qquad
  \theta = \operatorname{atan2}\bigl(E_y - S_y,\; E_x - S_x\bigr).
  \label{eq:angle}
\end{equation}
Because rotation is folded into $T_V$, rotated pages need no special case: on a
page with \code{/Rotate 90}, $\theta$ simply comes out as a right angle.

Each word is then written as one invisible text object. With $w_{\text{nat}}$ the
word's natural width in Helvetica at size $h$, the horizontal scaling (operator
\code{Tz}, in percent) stretches or squeezes it to exactly $w$:
\begin{equation}
  s = \operatorname{clamp}\!\Bigl(100\,\frac{w}{w_{\text{nat}}},\; 1,\; 1000\Bigr),
  \qquad
  T_m = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta & 0\\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0\\ S_x & S_y & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
  \label{eq:tz}
\end{equation}
The resulting content-stream fragment is
\code{BT 3~Tr /F~$h$~Tf $s$~Tz $T_m$~Tm (word)~Tj ET}. Font size $h$ makes the
highlight as tall as the visible word, $s$ makes it as wide, and $T_m$ puts it
in the same place at the same angle.

Existing PDF text lives in user space already (PDF.js returns each item's
transform), so de-duplication compares like with like: an OCR word is dropped
when the midpoint of its baseline, $(S+E)/2$, falls inside an existing text item's
box \emph{and} that item's text contains the word. Different text at the same
spot---for example a caption baked into an image next to real text---is kept.

\subsection{Key Design Decisions}
Seven decisions shape the rest of the system. Several reverse earlier choices,
and Section~\ref{sec:process} tells how each was reached.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Chrome's viewer is the only interface: change the file, not the
        viewer.} \label{dec:native}
        Chrome renders PDFs in a built-in viewer that extensions cannot script
        or extend. Earlier versions of \proj{} therefore re-rendered the PDF in
        a custom PDF.js viewer with an invisible DOM text layer. No DOM viewer
        can reproduce Chrome's (its toolbar, thumbnails, printing, Google~Lens,
        right-click menu, and above all its find bar), and an attempt to imitate
        it was rejected by the user. The final design gives Chrome a different
        \emph{file} instead---one that contains the text---so every pixel of
        interface is Chrome's own.
  \item \textbf{Never modify the original; write into a copy.} \label{dec:copy}
        The source file is downloaded and left untouched. Only a copy receives
        the invisible layer, and because mode-3 text paints nothing, the copy
        renders pixel-identically to the original. Invoking the extension again
        restores the original URL.
  \item \textbf{Activate on demand.} \label{dec:ondemand}
        The extension's value is real only for scanned and image PDFs, and OCR is
        expensive, so nothing happens until the user asks. Ordinary PDFs keep
        Chrome's default behaviour with zero overhead.
  \item \textbf{Serve the copy from the extension's origin, not from a
        \code{blob:} URL.} \label{dec:serve}
        The first version opened the copy as a \code{blob:} URL. In real Chrome
        that navigation never completes: an extension's blob URL cannot be
        opened from an ordinary website tab, consistent with Chrome's
        partitioning of blob URLs by site~\cite{blobpartition}. The copy is
        therefore stored in Cache Storage and served by the service worker's
        \code{fetch} handler at
        \code{chrome-extension://<id>/searchable/<token>/<file name>?src=<original>}.
        Ending the path in the original file name keeps the tab title; the
        \code{src} parameter makes restoring trivial and lets an expired copy
        fall back to the original instead of an error page.
  \item \textbf{Run the pipeline in a hidden document.} \label{dec:offscreen}
        Rendering and OCR need a DOM and Web Workers, which service workers lack,
        and must not disturb the tab. An offscreen document provides both, and
        its PDF.js rendering exists purely for OCR (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).
  \item \textbf{Identify documents by content.} \label{dec:hash}
        OCR results are cached by the SHA-256 of the file's bytes rather than its
        URL. The same file reached through two URLs is recognized once, and a
        changed file at the same URL (an edited local file, a ``latest.pdf''
        link) can never receive stale words.
  \item \textbf{Keep a long-lived background component bounded.}
        \label{dec:bounded}
        The offscreen document can live for the whole browser session, so
        every resource it creates has an owner and an end: pdf.js documents are
        destroyed after each job, OCR workers are released after a minute idle,
        copies are deleted when their tab closes or navigates away (and at
        browser start-up), and the OCR cache is pruned to about 2{,}000 pages.
\end{enumerate}
